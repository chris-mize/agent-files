# Evidence Rubric

Use a topic-specific matrix. Do not rank evidence by official status, credentials, crowd size, popularity, or rhetorical confidence alone.

## Claim ontology

Classify each material statement as one of these tiers:

1. **Observed evidence**: directly measured, fetched, witnessed, or reported experience.
2. **Source claim**: what a source asserts, including its definitions and stated limits.
3. **Corroborated pattern**: a recurring pattern supported by independent sources or appropriately convergent experiences.
4. **Expert interpretation**: a mechanism, model, forecast, or synthesis offered by a knowledgeable person.
5. **Competing explanation or option**: plausible alternatives, disagreements, and trade-offs.
6. **Action guidance**: an option for the user, with expected benefits, risks, costs, and conditions.

Do not silently upgrade a source claim to fact, a repeated anecdote to prevalence, or an expert opinion to consensus.

## Evidence dimensions

Assess each consequential claim across the dimensions that fit the topic:

- directness and proximity to the underlying event;
- method quality and transparency;
- independence and corroboration;
- sample quality, selection effects, and missing denominator;
- replication or reproducibility;
- recency and relevance to the user's scope;
- track record and predictive accuracy where applicable;
- incentives, funding, marketing, liability, or conflicts;
- lived-experience relevance and practical specificity;
- actionability and the cost of being wrong.

Use qualitative labels such as `strong`, `moderate`, `limited`, `contested`, and `unknown`, with a short reason. Do not collapse the dimensions into a single score unless the topic genuinely requires ranking options; if ranking, show the dimensions and weights.

Every consequential claim in the normalized ledger retains an evidence-quality label, the applicable dimensions with reasons, and a claim-specific confidence label. The parent may revise a worker's preliminary assessment, but must preserve the reason and any disagreement.

## Lane-specific interpretation

### Official and primary sources

Value their definitions, measurements, records, standards, and institutional knowledge. Also record mandate, publication purpose, funding, public-relations incentives, liability posture, excluded populations, and what the source is not designed to answer.

### User experiences

Value lived failure modes, edge cases, practical variance, usability, and time-to-outcome. Check selection effects, review bombing, survivorship bias, repeated anecdotes, copied text, missing denominators, geography, and product/version differences. Report recurring patterns without claiming they establish causation.

### Independent experts

Value demonstrated work, technical depth, long-term first-hand experience, methods, track record, predictive accuracy, peer/community reputation, and explanatory power. Include independent critics and armchair experts when their reasoning and evidence merit attention. Record credentials as one signal and disclose incentives or conflicts.

## Discrepancy analysis

When lanes disagree, preserve the disagreement and test:

- different populations, geographies, time periods, or use cases;
- different definitions or endpoints;
- institutional publication, marketing, or liability incentives;
- user selection, reporting, or denominator effects;
- expert school, method, or forecast differences;
- genuine uncertainty or changing conditions.

State whether the disagreement is resolved, partially explained, or irreducible. Surface minority views when relevant and evidenced, even when they do not win the synthesis.

## Vague guidance audit

Flag recommendations that only say “be careful,” “consult an expert,” “follow best practices,” or equivalent. Identify the missing decision, threshold, mechanism, evidence, or next action. Search for operational alternatives. If the evidence cannot support greater specificity, say exactly why instead of filling the gap with invented certainty.

## Confidence

Confidence is claim-specific. It reflects evidence quality and convergence, not source count alone:

- `high`: direct, methodologically credible, independently corroborated, and scope-matched;
- `moderate`: useful support with material limitations or incomplete convergence;
- `low`: plausible but weak, indirect, selected, or sparsely supported;
- `contested`: credible evidence points in materially different directions;
- `unknown`: insufficient accessible evidence.
